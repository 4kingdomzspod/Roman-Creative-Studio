# Objection Handling System
## Roman Creative Studio — Phase 5, Document 7

---

### The Nature of Objections

An objection is not a rejection. It is a request for more information, reassurance, or reframing.

When a prospect says "it's too expensive," they are usually saying one of the following:
- "I don't yet understand why it's worth that."
- "I'm scared of making a bad investment."
- "I've been burned before and I'm protecting myself."
- "I don't have budget authorization and need help justifying it internally."

None of these require a discount. They require a more effective value conversation.

**The governing rule:** Objections are reframed into value conversations, never into discounts.

---

## Section 1 — Objection Response Framework

Every objection response follows the same four-step structure:

```
1. ACKNOWLEDGE     — Validate without agreeing
2. CLARIFY         — Understand the real concern beneath the stated objection
3. REFRAME         — Shift the frame from cost to value / risk to opportunity
4. BRIDGE          — Move toward next step
```

## Section 2 — Core Objections

### Objection 1: "It's too expensive."

**What they usually mean:**
- "I'm not convinced the ROI is there."
- "I don't have this in my current budget."
- "I wasn't expecting that number."

**Response:**
```
"I hear you — it's a real investment. Can I ask: is it too expensive
in absolute terms, or does the return on investment feel unclear?"
```

*(Pause. Let them answer. The answer tells you how to respond.)*

**If ROI is unclear:**
```
"Let me put this in a business context.

For a [their industry] business with [X] average client/patient/customer value,
if this website improvement produces even a 20% increase in qualified
inquiries per month, that's [calculated monthly revenue increase].

The investment for this project is $[price]. At that return, you recover
the cost in [X months] — and every month after that is net gain.

Does that math make sense for your business?"
```

**If genuinely over budget:**
```
"I appreciate you being straight with me. I won't reduce the investment
on this scope — it's priced at what it actually takes to deliver the result.

What I can do: let's look at what we'd remove to get it to a number
that works. What's the most critical thing you need from this project?"
```

*Offer a reduced scope, not a discounted price.*

---

### Objection 2: "We need to think about it."

**What they usually mean:**
- "I have an unspoken concern I haven't voiced."
- "I need internal approval."
- "I'm comparing other options."
- "I'm not ready to commit."

**Response:**
```
"Absolutely — this is a significant decision and I want you to feel confident.

Can I ask: what specifically do you need to think through? Is it the
investment, the timing, or something about the approach itself?"
```

*(The key is to surface the real concern. "We need to think about it" is almost never the real objection.)*

**If it's budget:**
See Objection 1.

**If it's internal approval:**
```
"That makes sense. What would help most — a shorter summary I can put
together for your [partner/board/team] to review, or a call where I
can answer their questions directly?"
```

**If it's comparing options:**
```
"Of course — you should be informed. What are the key things you'll
be comparing? I'd rather be helpful in that process than just
hope you come back to us."

*(Then address the comparison criteria directly. If they're comparing
on price, return to the ROI frame. If they're comparing on approach,
emphasize the RCS methodology differentiation.)*
```

---

### Objection 3: "We'll get back to you."

**What they usually mean:**
- Polite way to end the conversation without deciding
- Something objectionable they haven't voiced
- Genuine process requirement

**Response:**
```
"Of course — I want to make sure we use the time well though.

When you say you'll get back to me — is there a specific date by
which you'd want to make a decision on this? I want to make sure
I hold your timeline."
```

*(Establishing a date creates accountability without pressure.)*

**If they give a date:** Schedule a follow-up call on that date. Not an email — a call.

**If they won't give a date:**
```
"That's okay. What I'll do is follow up on [specific date] to check in.
If circumstances have changed, just let me know — I'd rather know
than wonder."
```

---

### Objection 4: "We already have a website."

**What they usually mean:**
- "Why should I spend money when I already have something?"
- "The sunk cost feels like a reason to stay."

**Response:**
```
"You do — and I want to be straight with you: having a website and
having a website that works are two different things.

From what you told me earlier, your current site is [specific problem
they mentioned — not converting, not ranking, outdated]. That means
you're already paying the cost of the problem — just in missed leads
instead of in an investment.

The question isn't whether to spend money. It's whether to keep losing
$[estimated monthly lead loss] quietly or to invest $[proposal amount]
to stop the leak."
```

---

### Objection 5: "Can you do it cheaper?"

**What they usually mean:**
- They want to buy — but are testing your confidence in the price
- They've been trained to negotiate
- Budget is genuinely constrained

**Response (confident, non-apologetic):**
```
"I can't reduce the price on this scope — it's built to what the
work actually requires.

What I can do is look at reducing the scope to fit a different budget.
But I want to be clear: cutting the investment means cutting the
deliverables, which means a smaller result.

What number are you working with? Let's see what's possible."
```

**Never apologize for your pricing. Never drop the price without dropping scope.**

---

### Objection 6: "Your competitor charges less."

**Response:**
```
"They might. And that's a valid option if price is the deciding factor.

What I'd ask you to consider: what specifically will be different
between their output and ours? Is it the strategy depth? The
conversion architecture? The industry-specific expertise?

You can always get a cheaper website. The question is whether a
cheaper website actually solves the problem you described — because
if it doesn't, you'll be having this conversation again in 18 months."
```

---

### Objection 7: "Can we start smaller and expand later?"

**What they usually mean:**
- Want to test the relationship before committing fully
- Budget is genuinely limited
- This is a legitimate pathway to a bigger engagement

**Response:**
```
"Absolutely — and that's a reasonable approach.

Here's what I'd suggest: let's define a Phase 1 scope that solves
your most critical problem and produces a measurable result. If
that goes well — and it will — Phase 2 is an easy conversation.

What's the single most important outcome you need from Phase 1?"
```

*This is the one situation where a smaller scope is offered proactively — because it's genuinely a Phase 1/Phase 2 structure, not a discount.*

---

### Objection 8: "We've had bad experiences with agencies before."

**What they usually mean:**
- Fear of repeating a painful experience
- Skepticism about agency promises
- Testing whether RCS will overpromise

**Response:**
```
"I'm not surprised — it's unfortunately common. What happened?"
```

*(Listen fully. Don't interrupt. Don't defend the industry.)*

```
"That sounds frustrating. What I can tell you is what's structurally
different about how we work:

[Specific difference #1 — e.g., fixed scope in writing, no scope creep]
[Specific difference #2 — e.g., payment tied to milestones, not upfront]
[Specific difference #3 — e.g., documented process with defined approvals]

I can't guarantee a different experience — but I can show you a
different process. Would it help to see our project agreement before
you decide?"
```

## Section 3 — Objection Handling Rules

1. **Never interrupt an objection.** Let the prospect finish completely.
2. **Never get defensive.** An objection is not an attack. Treat it like a question.
3. **Never discount to close.** It devalues the work and signals a lack of confidence.
4. **Always clarify before responding.** Ask "Is it X or Y?" — make sure you're responding to the real concern.
5. **Use silence strategically.** After a reframe, pause. Let it land before saying more.
6. **Know when to let go.** Not every objection is handleable. If the prospect is fundamentally not aligned with premium value, gracefully disengage.

---

## Section 4 — The Objection Prevention Strategy

The best objection handling happens before the objection occurs.

Objections in the proposal stage are often symptoms of gaps in the discovery call:

| Objection | Root Cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| "Too expensive" | ROI wasn't established in discovery | Use the ROI frame in every discovery call |
| "Need to think about it" | Unstated concern not surfaced | Ask better discovery questions |
| "We'll get back to you" | No urgency established | Identify the cost of inaction in discovery |
| "Already have a website" | Didn't frame the existing site as a liability | Include website audit in discovery |
| "Can you do it cheaper?" | Price wasn't anchored or framed properly | Anchor pricing in the discovery call |
