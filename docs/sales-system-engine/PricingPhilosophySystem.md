# Pricing Philosophy System
## Roman Creative Studio — Phase 5, Document 5

---

### The Core Pricing Principle

RCS prices on **value delivered**, not hours spent.

Hourly pricing is a freelancer model. It penalizes efficiency, caps earning potential, and frames the relationship as time-for-money rather than outcome-for-investment. The faster RCS gets at delivering great work, the less hourly pricing would pay. That is structurally wrong.

Value-based pricing asks: *What is the measurable business outcome this work will produce, and what is a fair portion of that value to exchange for producing it?*

A dental practice that gets a new website and increases new patient inquiries by 30% is gaining tens of thousands of dollars per year in revenue. The investment in that website is justified against that number — not against "how many hours it took to design."

## Section 1 — Pricing Philosophy Rules

### Rule 1: No Hourly Pricing

Never quote an hourly rate. Never break down pricing by hours internally or externally. Hours are an input. Outcomes are the product. The client is buying the outcome.

### Rule 2: No Discounting

Discounting signals one of two things:
1. The original price was inflated (deceptive)
2. The work isn't worth the stated price (weakness)

Neither message serves RCS. When a prospect asks for a discount, the response is always a scope reduction, not a price reduction.

> "I can't reduce the investment on this scope, but I can adjust the scope to fit your budget. What would you prioritize removing?"

This preserves price integrity while giving the prospect a path forward.

### Rule 3: Pricing is Presented with Confidence

The way pricing is delivered signals whether the agency believes in its own value.

| Weak delivery | Strong delivery |
|---|---|
| "It would be around... maybe $8,000 or so?" | "The investment for this project is $9,500." |
| "I can probably do it for less if needed" | "That's the investment. Here's what it gets you." |
| "Our prices start at..." | "Based on what you've described, the right scope is X at $Y." |

State the price. Pause. Let it land. Do not fill the silence with justification.

### Rule 4: Price is Anchored to Outcome

Every pricing conversation must connect the investment to a specific business outcome:

```
"A well-built website for a dental practice in a competitive market
typically results in a 20–40% increase in new patient inquiries.
For a practice averaging $800 per patient, that's $X in additional
annual revenue from this investment."
```

When the math is laid out, the investment looks different.

### Rule 5: The Scope Defines the Price — Not the Client's Budget

RCS builds the right scope for the problem, then prices it accurately. The prospect's stated budget is a data point — not the ceiling.

If the right solution costs $12,000 and the prospect says their budget is $6,000, the answer is not to deliver a $12,000 solution for $6,000. The answer is either:
- Reduce scope to match the budget honestly (what can we build for $6,000?)
- Help the prospect understand why the full solution is worth the investment
- Decline the project if neither path works

## Section 3 — Anchor Pricing Strategy

### 3.1 What Anchoring Does

The first number a prospect hears becomes the reference point for all subsequent numbers. This is psychological anchoring. Used correctly, it frames your actual price as reasonable or even affordable.

**Anchoring in practice:**
```
"Agencies at the full-service end of the market charge $30,000–$80,000
for what you're describing. We're not there. For our scope, the investment
is $12,500 — with the same level of strategy and execution."
```

The prospect hears $30,000–$80,000 first. $12,500 now sounds accessible.

### 3.2 Internal Anchor Rules

- Always present the highest tier or most comprehensive option first in a proposal
- Let the prospect move down in scope, not up
- Never lead with your lowest option (it anchors to the bottom)

### 3.3 The Three-Option Principle

When presenting multiple tiers, the middle option is almost always chosen. The top option exists to anchor value. The bottom option exists to prevent loss of the deal.

```
Option A (Authority):   $XX,XXX   ← Anchor. Makes Option B look reasonable.
Option B (Growth):      $XX,XXX   ← Recommended. The right solution.
Option C (Foundation):  $X,XXX    ← Floor. Preserves the relationship if budget is real.
```

## Section 4 — Value Justification Framework

When presenting pricing, always connect it to a business calculation:

### Template: The ROI Frame

```
"Let's think about what this is worth to your business.

Right now, your website is generating roughly [X leads/month].
A well-built conversion-focused site in your industry typically
produces a [20–40%] improvement in lead volume.

For a [dental practice/healthcare provider/service business] with
an average client value of $[Y], that's an additional $[Z] per year.

The investment for this project is $[price].
At that return, you're looking at payback in [X months]."
```

You are not justifying the price. You are helping them see the math that makes the decision obvious.

### When You Don't Have Their Numbers

Use industry benchmarks:
- Dental: average new patient value $800–$2,000/year
- Healthcare: average new patient value $500–$3,000/year
- Restaurant: average cover value $40–80, customer LTV multiple visits
- Church: average giving member contributes $X/year (varies widely)
- Service business: client LTV $1,000–20,000+ depending on service

## Section 5 — How Pricing is Presented

### In the Discovery Call

Introduce a price range (anchor) to test budget alignment before investing in proposal writing:
```
"Projects like this typically range from $[low] to $[high].
Based on your situation, I'd estimate we're in the $[middle] range.
Is that territory you can work with?"
```

### In the Proposal

Present pricing as "Investment" — never "Cost" or "Price."
- "Cost" frames the transaction as an expense
- "Investment" frames it as a business decision with expected return

Proposal format:
```
INVESTMENT SUMMARY

Total Investment:  $XX,XXX
Payment Structure: 50% to begin / 25% at design approval / 25% at launch
Timeline:          X weeks from signed agreement
```

### The Pause

After stating the investment, pause. Do not immediately justify, defend, or add more information. Let the number sit.

If the prospect is silent: they are processing, not objecting. Most salespeople fill this silence with unnecessary discounting. Don't.

If they respond positively: move to next steps.
If they hesitate: ask "What's your reaction to that?" — let them name the objection.
